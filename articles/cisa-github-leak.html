What the CISA GitHub Leak Actually Exposed

bottom line up front

The agency that writes the playbook for everyone else just proved, in public, that the playbook only works if it's actually followed. Every organization running developer environments, contractor access, or cloud infrastructure should treat this less as a story about CISA and more as a mirror.

For six months, a public GitHub repository quietly held the keys, literally, to some of the federal government's most sensitive cloud infrastructure. It wasn't discovered by an attacker nor CISA. It was discovered by a security researcher from GitGuardian running routine, automated scans, who then had to escalate through a journalist because the agency's own reporting channels weren't clear enough to reach the right people quickly. [1]

That's the real headline buried under the technical details: CISA, the agency that writes the federal government's own rules for cyber hygiene, ended up as living proof of what happens when nobody enforces them.

The Exposure

A contractor working for CISA had uploaded a copy of the agency's build and deployment repository to a personal GitHub account, intending to spin up cloud infrastructure independently. In the process, administrator and build credentials for CISA's development systems, along with infrastructure-as-code data describing how the agency's cloud environment is configured, ended up in that same public folder.

The repository, ironically named "Private-CISA," contained roughly 844 megabytes of material: plaintext passwords, AWS access tokens, CI/CD (Continuous Integration/Continuous Deployment) build logs, Kubernetes manifests, and Terraform infrastructure code. One file was titled, without apparent irony, "importantAWStokens." It held administrative credentials to three AWS GovCloud servers: the isolated cloud environment used for regulated government workloads.

GitHub ships with a default setting that blocks users from publishing SSH keys and other secrets into public repositories. According to the CISA postmortem, the account holder had explicitly disabled it. The safety mechanism wasn't bypassed through some clever exploit: it was turned off, presumably for convenience.

A Rare Moment of Honesty and Why It's Not Enough

Credit where it's due: CISA's after-action report, published in July, was unusually candid. [2] The agency acknowledged that its private repositories also contained secrets that had to be removed and rotated. It admitted that researcher-reporting channels weren't well defined, forcing the researcher who discovered the issue to try email, a vulnerability disclosure platform meant for different kinds of reports, and finally a journalist, before the issue got real traction. That level of self-critique from a national cybersecurity agency is rare. [3] [4]

But transparency after the fact doesn't undo the exposure window. This wasn't a sophisticated adversary defeating layered defenses. It was a known, well-documented failure mode: static secrets committed to a personal repository that has caused breaches for a decade across every industry. The lesson isn't really about CISA's incompetence. It's about what happens when contractor oversight, credential architecture, and detection response all rely on manual diligence rather than structural enforcement.

It's worth noting that CISA has lost roughly a third of its workforce amid recent budget and staffing reductions. That doesn't excuse the exposure. But it's a useful data point for any organization asking a harder question: do our security controls actually hold up when headcount shrinks and contractors turn over? Most controls quietly depend on people paying continuous attention, and that's exactly what's hardest to sustain when a team is stretched thin.

This repository incident isn't the only recent episode raising questions about discipline at the top of the agency. In January, Politico reported that CISA's own acting director, Madhu Gottumukkala, had uploaded government contracting documents marked "for official use only" to the public version of ChatGPT between mid-July and early August 2025. The uploads tripped CISA's own automated security sensors, generating multiple alerts and prompting a DHS review into whether the disclosures had compromised government security. The files weren't classified, but they weren't meant for public release either, and once entered into a public LLM they're subject to the platform's own data handling, outside federal control entirely. What made it worse: Gottumukkala had personally requested and received a special exception to use ChatGPT months earlier, at a time when the tool was blocked for most DHS staff over exactly this kind of risk. One official told Politico he'd pushed CISA into granting him access, "and then he abused it." [5]

Put the two incidents side by side and a pattern emerges that's more troubling than either one alone. A leaked secret in a personal repo is a process failure anyone can have. A sitting agency head bypassing his own organization's data-handling controls, the ones his agency sets for the rest of the federal government, is a culture failure.

How a Secrets Leak Actually Unfolds

Understanding why this kind of exposure is so dangerous requires understanding two mechanics that run in parallel.

First, detection. Firms like GitGuardian continuously scan public code repositories: not just an organization's own GitHub account, but the entire public internet, looking for patterns that match password formats, API key structures, and other secret signatures. It functions like a smoke detector for the open internet: always listening, flagging anomalies automatically. In this case, the system worked. Automated alerts were generated well before the public disclosure. The failure wasn't detection; it was response: the alerts didn't reach the right people fast enough to act.

Second, blast radius. A single exposed administrative key doesn't just expose one system. It exposes every downstream resource that key can touch. In a cloud environment built on interconnected roles and permissions, the impact can be substantial. That's why the remediation timeline matters as much as the discovery timeline. Even after CISA took the repository offline, the exposed AWS access keys reportedly remained technically valid for an additional 48 hours, because fully rotating credentials across a complex, interconnected environment takes real operational time: time an attacker doesn't need to wait for.

This is the core argument for moving away from static, long-lived credentials entirely. A permanent access key is a liability the moment it's created, because its value to an attacker never depreciates until someone manually revokes it. A short-lived credential that expires automatically within minutes is the best practice. If it leaks, it's already useless by the time anyone finds it.

What Security Teams Should Actually Do

  • Audit immediately. Search for personal or unmanaged accounts holding company infrastructure-as-code, build scripts, or credential files. This should happen this week, not next quarter.
  • Lock down push protection. Confirm that secret-scanning and push-protection settings on your organization's repositories cannot be disabled by an individual contributor or contractor account.
  • Move to dynamic secrets. Begin migrating static cloud credentials, the kind that don't expire, toward short-lived, dynamically issued tokens scoped to specific roles and sessions.
  • Extend scanning to contractors. Contractual language should require continuous, automated secrets scanning across any repository a contractor controls, not just repositories the organization directly owns.
  • Define your disclosure channel. Publish a single, clear, prominently placed channel for security researchers to report exposures, and make sure it's monitored by someone who can act same-day, not next-quarter.
  • Rotate as a drill, not a crisis. Treat credential rotation like a fire drill: scheduled, rehearsed, and boring, not something improvised under pressure after a public leak.
